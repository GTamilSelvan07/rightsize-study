Proposed additions

Seven survey items and three story beats, drafted to your ~10-minute ceiling. Nothing here is wired in yet. Wording is in the house voice already used in index.html — plain, concrete, no jargon.

Part 1 — Survey items

Six single-select plus one optional free-text. Estimated added burden: 2–3 minutes. Placed in the three sections that are currently thinnest for analysis.

New · section C (The annoying parts) · c_authority · single select

When a client asks for something new mid-project, how clear is it who can say yes to a new date or a new cost?

  • Always clear — one named person decides
  • Usually clear
  • Depends on the client
  • Usually unclear — it gets worked out as we go
  • Never clear

Why this one first. Your whole story turns on the line “Nobody said who can say yes to a new date, a new cost, or a safety check.” Right now nothing in the survey measures that. This item is the quantitative counterpart to the narrative, and it is the variable most likely to separate the people who need your idea from the people who do not.

New · section C · c_quick_request_rework · single select

How often does a request that sounded small turn into a week or more of extra work?

  • Most months
  • Every few months
  • Once or twice a year
  • Almost never

Gives you a base rate for the exact failure the story dramatises, so you can say how common it is rather than only that it happens.

New · section B (AI at work) · b_verify · single select

When an AI tool gives you something you will act on, what do you do first?

  • Check it against the source myself
  • Skim it for anything obviously wrong
  • Use it as-is if it looks reasonable
  • Use it as-is
  • I don’t act on AI output

Trust calibration. Your four helpers are defined by their limits (“can’t decide anything”, “can’t approve anything”), and this tells you whether people already behave as if limits matter — or whether the limits are solving a problem they don’t think they have.

New · section B · b_allow_without_human · multi select

Which of these would you let an AI do without a person approving it first?

  • Gather facts and show where they came from
  • Write a first draft of a message
  • Send a message to a client
  • Flag that someone is missing from a decision
  • Record what was agreed
  • Change a date or a cost
  • None of these

This is the one that directly tests your design. The list is deliberately your four helpers’ jobs plus two things they are not allowed to do. If people already draw the line where you drew it, that is a finding; if they draw it somewhere else, that is a bigger one.

New · section D (The idea) · d_would_use · single select

If this existed today, how likely would you be to use it on your next project?

  • Very likely · Likely · Not sure · Unlikely · Very unlikely

Section D presents the idea and currently collects one input, so there is no clean measure of reaction to it. A five-point intention item is the minimum needed to compare reactions across the profile questions in section A.

New · section D · d_blocker · single select

What would most likely stop you using it?

  • My clients wouldn’t agree to it
  • My team wouldn’t keep it up
  • It’s another tool to run
  • I don’t trust AI with anything that matters
  • Cost
  • Nothing — I’d try it

Turns a flat “no” into a reason you can act on. Single-select forces the strongest blocker rather than letting people tick everything.

New · section D · d_blocker_other · free text, optional, 300 chars

Anything else that would stop you? (optional)

The only free-text item added, and optional, so it costs nothing in completion rate. Catches the blocker your six options missed.

Part 2 — Story beats

Three new decision points for assets/js/scenes.js, following the existing { type: "choice", key, log, prompt, options } shape. Each adds roughly 20–30 seconds.

New · Act 1, before the alignment meeting · act1_c0

It’s 4:56 pm. Before you reply to Rowan, who do you tell?

  • Nobody yet — reply first, sort the details Monday
  • Priya, so she can look at the plan
  • Sam, because sign-in means a safety check
  • Everyone, in the project channel

Currently Act 1 goes straight from the request to the eight-person meeting. This beat makes the reader commit before they see the cost, so the meeting lands as a consequence of their own choice instead of something that merely happens to them. Every option converges, exactly like act1_c1 already does.

New · Act 2, after Prep reports · act2_limit

Prep has the facts and a draft reply ready. Rowan is waiting. What do you do?

  • Send Prep’s draft as it stands
  • Read it, change it, then send it yourself
  • Ask Sam to confirm the safety line first

The first option should be unavailable — shown, then refused with Prep’s own limit: “Can’t decide anything.” That is the most persuasive thirty seconds you can give a sceptical reader: they try to over-trust the AI and the design stops them. It also gives you a click-level measure of how many people try.

New · Act 2 close, after the receipt · act2_promise

Three weeks on, Promise reports what actually happened. It doesn’t match what was agreed.

  • Update the record and tell Rowan
  • Update the record, no message
  • Leave it — the work is done

Promise is described as “checks the outcome later” but never actually does so on screen, so the fourth helper is the only one the reader never sees work. This closes that gap and ends on the study’s real claim: the value is the floor under what happens next, not the meeting.

Layout across devices

The grid was a fixed 3 columns, 2 on phones — so two role cards filled two thirds of a row, four helpers broke 3 + 1, and on a phone two cards shared the width and wrapped to two or three words a line. Now auto-fit with a per-grid floor: role cards 2 across, roster 3 across, helpers 2 × 2, all collapsing to one column on a phone.
